Foundations

1. What marketing is

Marketing is the process of identifying customers' needs and wants and satisfying them profitably — through products, services and experiences that create value. It is much broader than selling or advertising.

Marketing starts before production (finding out what customers need) and continues after the sale (service, feedback and repeat purchases).

Need

A basic human requirement — food, clothing, shelter.

Want

A need shaped by culture and personality — e.g. a branded pair of shoes.

Demand

A want backed by the ability and willingness to pay.

Selling vs marketing

Basis Selling Marketing
Starting point An existing product Customer needs
Scope Transfer of goods to the buyer The whole business activity
Aim Maximise sales volume Customer satisfaction and profit
Approach Push products on customers Pull customers towards the product

The core framework

2. The marketing mix — 4 Ps

The marketing mix is the set of decisions a firm makes to satisfy its target market. For goods, these are the classic 4 Ps.

P What it covers Question to ask
Product Features, quality, design, brand, packaging, service What exactly are we offering?
Price List price, discounts, credit terms, allowances What is it worth to the buyer?
Place Channels, coverage, locations, logistics How does it reach the customer?
Promotion Advertising, sales promotion, selling, PR How do we communicate and persuade?

Services add three more Ps

  • People — the staff who deliver the service shape the experience.
  • Process — how smoothly the service is delivered.
  • Physical evidence — the environment and tangible cues that signal quality.

Strategy

3. Segmentation, targeting and positioning

STP is how a firm decides whom to serve. Segment the market, target the best-fit groups, then position the brand in the customer's mind. 

Segmentation  →  divide the market into groups with similar needs
Targeting     →  choose which segment(s) to serve
Positioning   →  create a distinct place for the brand in the customer's mind

Common bases of segmentation

Base Examples
Demographic Age, gender, income, education, occupation, family stage
Geographic Region, city size, climate, urban vs rural
Psychographic Lifestyle, personality, values, interests
Behavioural Usage rate, loyalty, benefits sought, occasion

Targeting strategies

  • Undifferentiated — one offer for the whole market.
  • Differentiated — separate offers for several segments.
  • Concentrated — focus on one niche segment.
  • Micromarketing — tailor to individuals or local groups.

Positioning statement: for [target segment], our brand is the [category] that [point of difference]. A car brand that advertises safety is positioning on safety.

The customer

4. Consumer behaviour

Consumer behaviour studies how people decide what to buy, when and where. It covers both the factors that shape the decision and the steps of the decision itself.

The five-step buying process

1. Need recognition
2. Information search
3. Evaluation of alternatives
4. Purchase decision
5. Post-purchase behaviour

Factors that influence buyers

Cultural

Culture, subculture and social class.

Social

Family, friends, reference groups and roles.

Personal

Age, occupation, income, lifestyle and personality.

Psychological

Motivation, perception, learning, beliefs and attitudes.

Post-purchase matters: a satisfied buyer returns and recommends; a disappointed one complains, returns the product and talks about it. Good marketers follow up and fix problems quickly.

The first P

5. Product, branding and the product life cycle

Three levels of a product

  • Core benefit — the problem it solves (a phone keeps you connected).
  • Actual product — features, design, quality, brand name and packaging.
  • Augmented product — warranty, after-sales service, delivery and installation.

Product life cycle

Stage Sales and profit Typical focus
Introduction Low sales, losses Build awareness; choose skimming or penetration pricing
Growth Rising sales and profits Improve quality, expand distribution, build loyalty
Maturity Peak sales, intense competition Differentiate, promote heavily, find new uses
Decline Falling sales and profits Harvest the brand or withdraw it

Branding, packaging and labelling

A brand is a name, sign, symbol or design that identifies a product and sets it apart. The spoken part is the brand name, the symbol is the brand mark, and a legally protected brand is a trademark.

Packaging protects the product, aids identification and convenience, and promotes it on the shelf. Labelling gives information, warnings, ingredients, dates and statutory details.

The second P

6. Pricing decisions

Price is the money a customer pays for the value received. It is the only P that creates revenue — the others create cost.

Factors affecting price

  • Cost — the floor below which a firm cannot survive.
  • Demand — how sensitive buyers are to price.
  • Competition — rivals' prices and offers.
  • Objectives — profit, sales, market share or prestige.
  • Government and legal rules — taxes and price controls.
Method / strategy Idea
Cost-plus pricing Add a standard markup to the cost of the product.
Marginal cost pricing Price covers variable cost and contributes to fixed costs.
Break-even pricing Set a price where total revenue equals total cost.
Perceived-value pricing Base the price on what customers believe the product is worth.
Penetration pricing Low introductory price to win market share quickly.
Skimming pricing High introductory price to recover costs from early adopters.
Psychological pricing ₹199 instead of ₹200 — price feels lower than it is.
Discriminatory pricing Different prices for different segments, places or times.

The third P

7. Place and distribution

Place covers everything that moves a product from the producer to the final buyer: channels, intermediaries, storage and transport.

Levels of distribution channels

Level Route Example
Zero Manufacturer → Consumer Factory outlet or the brand's own website
One Manufacturer → Retailer → Consumer Buying clothes directly from a local store
Two Manufacturer → Wholesaler → Retailer → Consumer Everyday groceries and FMCG products
Three Manufacturer → Agent → Wholesaler → Retailer → Consumer Agricultural produce and some industrial goods

What intermediaries do

They break bulk, collect an assortment of products, store goods, arrange transport, offer credit and pass on market information. They reduce the number of transactions a manufacturer would otherwise need.

Logistics covers transportation, warehousing, inventory management and order processing. Retail formats include department stores, supermarkets, specialty stores, convenience stores, e-commerce marketplaces and direct selling.

The fourth P

8. Promotion and advertising

Promotion communicates value and persuades customers to act. It is a mix of tools, not a single decision.

Tool What it is Strength
Advertising Paid, non-personal communication by an identified sponsor Mass reach, brand building
Sales promotion Short-term incentives — coupons, discounts, free samples Quick response
Personal selling Face-to-face conversation with a prospective buyer Persuasive, builds relationships
Publicity Unpaid coverage in the media Credible, low cost
Public relations Building goodwill with the public and stakeholders Trust and image

Advertising can be informative (introduce a product), persuasive (win customers from rivals) or reminder (keep a mature brand top of mind). 

AIDA model of persuasion
Attention → Interest → Desire → Action

Personal selling steps: prospecting, pre-approach, approach, presentation, handling objections, closing the sale and follow-up. Sales promotion tools include discounts, coupons, samples, contests, buy-one-get-one offers and loyalty programmes.

Evidence

9. Marketing research

Marketing research gathers and analyses information to help make marketing decisions — who buys, why, at what price and through which channel. 

1. Define the problem and objectives
2. Develop the research plan
3. Collect the data
4. Analyse the information
5. Report the findings
Basis Primary data Secondary data
Meaning Collected firsthand for this study Already collected by someone else
Examples Surveys, interviews, observation, experiments Reports, websites, government data, company records
Advantage Fits the exact research question Faster and usually cheaper
Limitation Time-consuming and costly May be old or collected for another purpose

Good questionnaires are short, use clear language, avoid leading questions and are tested on a small group first. A sample is the smaller group studied to draw conclusions about the whole population.

Responsibility

10. Marketing ethics and consumer protection

Persuasion is powerful, so marketing carries responsibilities. Ethical marketing gives accurate information, respects privacy and does not exploit vulnerable customers.

Common unethical practices

  • False or exaggerated claims about a product.
  • Hidden charges or misleading “before/after” prices.
  • Bait advertising — promoting a product that is not really available.
  • Pushing harmful products to children or using pressure tactics.
  • Misusing customer data collected online.

Consumer rights in India

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 protects buyers and covers e-commerce. Key rights include safety, the right to be informed, the right to choose, the right to be heard, the right to seek redressal and the right to consumer education.

Quick maths

11. Numbers you may need

Market share (%) = (Company sales ÷ Total market sales) × 100

Sales growth (%) = ((Current sales − Previous sales) ÷ Previous sales) × 100

Markup (%)   = ((Selling price − Cost) ÷ Cost) × 100

Margin (%)   = ((Selling price − Cost) ÷ Selling price) × 100

Example: if a company sells ₹40 lakh in a ₹5 crore market, its market share is (40 ÷ 500) × 100 = 8%.

Watch the wording: markup is calculated on cost, margin on selling price. The same profit gives different percentages.

12. Glossary

Marketing
Identifying and satisfying customer needs profitably through value-creating offerings.
Market
A set of actual and potential buyers of a product or service.
Needs
Basic human requirements such as food, clothing and shelter.
Wants
Needs shaped by culture, society and personality.
Demand
Wants backed by the ability and willingness to pay.
Market offering
A product, service, information or experience offered to satisfy a need.
Exchange
Obtaining something desired by offering something in return.
Customer value
The benefits a customer gets compared with the costs of buying.
Customer satisfaction
How well a product's perceived performance matches expectations.
Marketing management
Planning, organising, directing and controlling marketing activities.
Marketing mix
The 4 Ps — product, price, place and promotion.
Product
Anything offered to satisfy a need, including goods, services and ideas.
Price
The amount of money exchanged for a product or service.
Place
Making the product available at the right location and time.
Promotion
Communicating with customers to inform, persuade and remind.
Segmentation
Dividing a market into groups with similar needs or characteristics.
Targeting
Selecting the segment or segments the firm will serve.
Positioning
Creating a distinct place for a brand in the customer's mind.
Target market
The specific group of customers a marketing programme is aimed at.
USP
Unique selling proposition — the benefit that sets a brand apart from rivals.
Brand
A name, sign, symbol or design that identifies and differentiates a product.
Trademark
A brand or symbol legally protected from use by others.
Packaging
Designing the container that protects, identifies and promotes a product.
Labelling
Information printed on a package — contents, warnings, dates and directions.
Product life cycle
The stages a product passes through: introduction, growth, maturity and decline.
Marketing research
Systematic gathering and analysis of information for marketing decisions.
Primary data
Data collected firsthand for the specific study at hand.
Secondary data
Data already collected by someone else for another purpose.
Sample
A smaller group selected to represent the larger population in research.
Advertising
Paid, non-personal promotion by an identified sponsor.
Sales promotion
Short-term incentives such as discounts, coupons and free samples.
Personal selling
Face-to-face selling through conversation with a prospective buyer.
Publicity
Unpaid media coverage of a product or firm.
Public relations
Activities that build goodwill and a positive image with the public.
AIDA
Attention, Interest, Desire, Action — a model of how promotion persuades.
Channel of distribution
The path a product takes from producer to final consumer.
Wholesaler
An intermediary that buys in bulk and sells to retailers.
Retailer
An intermediary that sells directly to final consumers.
Logistics
Transportation, warehousing, inventory and order processing.
Digital marketing
Promotion and selling through online channels such as search, social media and email.
Consumer behaviour
The study of how people choose, buy, use and dispose of products.
Marketing environment
The micro and macro forces that affect marketing decisions.
SWOT analysis
A review of a firm's strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats.
Market share
A firm's sales as a percentage of total market sales.
Markup
The difference between selling price and cost, expressed as a percentage of cost.
Consumer rights
Protections for buyers, including safety, information, choice and redressal.
